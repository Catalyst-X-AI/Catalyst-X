A large share of professional identity is built on competence: the sense that a skill you developed over years is recognizably, durably yours. When AI systems begin performing tasks that were previously central to that identity — drafting, analyzing, deciding, even creating — the psychological effect goes beyond workload. It touches what motivation researchers, following self-determination theory, describe as core psychological needs at work: autonomy (control over how the work gets done), competence (a sense of mastery), and relatedness (feeling valued and connected to others through the work). AI adoption can unsettle all three at once if it is introduced without attention to how people experience it.

### The authorship and fairness question

Deloitte's 2026 research names this directly: AI increasingly blurs who authored a decision, an analysis, or a piece of work, which raises real questions about fairness, accountability, and credit. If a recommendation is AI-generated but a person is accountable for it, who is trusted, and who is blamed when it's wrong? Organizations that leave this ambiguous tend to see a quiet erosion of trust — not a single dramatic incident, but an accumulation of small, unresolved moments where accountability felt unclear.

### Why framing changes adoption outcomes

How AI is introduced matters as much as what is introduced. Positioning AI explicitly as augmentation — a tool that removes lower-value work so people can spend more time on judgment, relationships, and the parts of the job that required a human — tends to protect the sense of competence and autonomy described above. Positioning it primarily around headcount efficiency or cost reduction, even when true, tends to activate identity threat and defensive disengagement, which in turn undermines the very adoption the organization is trying to drive.
