Burnout is not simply "being tired." The World Health Organization classifies it in the ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon — specifically, a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed — with three defining dimensions: feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion; increased mental distance from one's job, or feelings of cynicism related to it; and reduced professional efficacy. It is a workplace condition, not a personal failing, and that distinction matters for how organizations should respond to it.

### What actually drives burnout

Research led by psychologist Christina Maslach — who developed the most widely used measure of burnout — points to six areas of "mismatch" between a person and their job that predict burnout risk: unsustainable workload, lack of control over how work gets done, insufficient reward or recognition, breakdown of community and collegial support, absence of fairness, and a values conflict between the person and the organization. Burnout rarely stems from a single cause; it accumulates where several of these mismatches overlap for a sustained period.

### Why this is a business metric, not just a wellbeing one

Burnout predicts absenteeism, turnover intention, error rates, and — particularly relevant in knowledge work — degraded decision quality, well before it shows up as a resignation. Organizations that only track burnout through exit interviews are measuring it after the cost has already been paid. Treating it as a leading indicator, tracked through the same mismatch factors Maslach identified, allows intervention while it is still cheap to address.
