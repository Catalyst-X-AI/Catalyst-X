Resilience through AI-era disruption is not a single trait people either have or lack — it is built, at both the individual and organizational level, from the specific factors this research points to repeatedly: a sense of control, confidence in one's ability to learn, psychological safety to raise concerns, and capable, present management.

### Individual-level levers

- **Self-efficacy with the tools themselves** — the strongest protective factor identified against AI-related stress; built through real, hands-on training, not a single onboarding session.
- **Self-leadership** — the ability to manage one's own focus, framing, and motivation through change, shown to buffer the strain of change fatigue even when the volume of change stays constant.
- **A sense of control** — even limited input into how a change is implemented (timing, sequencing, method) measurably reduces the psychological cost of the change itself, compared to change that is simply announced and imposed.

### Organizational-level levers

- **Manager enablement** — given that managers account for roughly 70% of engagement variance, investing in manager capability to support people through stress and change is the single highest-leverage lever available, and the one HR leaders report is currently weakest (90% say managers are not adequately equipped for this).
- **Transparent, honest communication about AI** — addressing individual-level uncertainty directly, rather than leading with aggregate "net jobs created" statistics that don't speak to any one person's actual risk.
- **Reducing total change load** — sequencing initiatives rather than running them concurrently, since change fatigue is a function of cumulative volume, not any single initiative's merit.
- **Human-centered AI rollout design** — treating "people are confident and trust the process" as a genuine project milestone, not an afterthought to the technical deployment.
