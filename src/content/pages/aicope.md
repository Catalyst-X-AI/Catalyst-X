### Name what's actually happening

Recent global research (Mercer's Global Talent Trends) found that 40% of workers now fear AI will make their job obsolete, up from 28% just two years earlier. If you feel this, you're describing a fast-growing, widely shared reaction — not a personal overreaction. Part of what makes it feel so unsettling is that this wave of change reaches into cognitive and knowledge work, the kind of expertise many people assumed was safely "theirs." That's a legitimate thing to feel uneasy about, and naming it accurately is the first step to responding to it deliberately rather than just absorbing the anxiety.

### Build the skills AI doesn't replace

A recurring finding in this research is that people who deliberately invest in distinctly human strengths — judgment calls in ambiguous situations, building trust and relationships, ethical reasoning, framing a problem in the first place — tend to feel more secure, because those strengths remain valuable regardless of which tools are in use. Pick one strength you already have and look for a concrete way to use it more deliberately this quarter, rather than treating your value as tied only to the specific tasks AI is now faster at.

### Craft your role, don't just endure it

Research on "job crafting" distinguishes two responses to a changing job: **approach crafting** — proactively reshaping your tasks and relationships to make the most of new tools — versus **avoidance crafting** — mentally withdrawing and disengaging. The research finds people who feel some sense of control over how AI enters their work engage in the first kind, while people who feel it's simply happening to them tend toward the second, which predicts lower engagement over time. A small, concrete example: volunteer to pilot a new tool so you help shape how it's used on your team, rather than waiting for it to be handed down.

### Protect your think time

Workplace mental health research points to a real risk in AI-accelerated environments: the pace can quietly ratchet up until there's no space left for the complex, careful thinking that is precisely where you add the most value. Protect that time deliberately — block it on your calendar, or say so plainly in a team conversation about workload — rather than letting it be the first thing that disappears.

### Have the career conversation early

Uncertainty is often harder to carry than bad news. Rather than waiting to see what happens, consider proactively asking your manager how your role's expectations and success measures are evolving as AI tools come in. Advocating for that clarity is itself a coping strategy, not just something to hope your organization gets around to.
