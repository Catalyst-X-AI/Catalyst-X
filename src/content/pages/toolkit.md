### 1. Start with self-compassion, not self-criticism

Psychologist Kristin Neff's research on self-compassion identifies three components that consistently support healthier coping: **self-kindness** (talking to yourself the way you'd talk to a friend having a hard week, not the way a harsh critic would), **common humanity** (remembering that struggling under real pressure is a shared human experience, not evidence you're uniquely failing), and **mindfulness** (noticing the stress without either suppressing it or being swallowed by it). People who practice this tend to recover from setbacks faster than people who default to self-criticism — not because they care less, but because they're not fighting the stress and a harsh inner voice at the same time.

### 2. Find your real mismatch

Burnout researcher Christina Maslach's work points to six specific areas where a mismatch between you and your job tends to build burnout: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values. Generic advice like "just relax more" often fails because it doesn't target your actual mismatch. Try naming, honestly, which one or two of these feel most strained for you right now — it's usually not all six, and knowing which ones lets you address something real instead of something generic.

### 3. Protect real recovery, not just downtime

Occupational health research on recovery from work stress (building on Sonnentag and Fritz's widely used framework) distinguishes passive downtime — scrolling, half-watching something — from experiences that actually restore you: genuine **psychological detachment** from work, true **relaxation**, a sense of **control** over your own time, and small experiences of **mastery** outside of work. A weekend can be full of "rest" and still leave you flat if it never included any of these. Pick one you're currently missing and protect it deliberately this week.

### 4. Set one boundary this week — not ten

Behavior-change research consistently favors one small, concrete commitment over a sweeping resolution that quietly gets abandoned by Wednesday. Pick a single boundary — a notification you turn off after a set time, a meeting you decline, a task you hand back — rather than trying to overhaul your entire relationship with work at once.

### 5. Know when to get more support

Everyday stress and clinical burnout exist on a spectrum, and it's worth being honest with yourself about where you are on it. If things feel persistent — trouble sleeping, a sense of dread most mornings, physical symptoms, or a loss of hope that it will get better — that's a signal to bring in more support than a self-help page can offer: a manager, HR, an employee assistance program, or a mental health professional.
