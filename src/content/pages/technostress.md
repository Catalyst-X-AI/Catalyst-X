"Technostress" is the established term in occupational psychology for stress caused specifically by the demands of new technology — commonly broken down into overload (too much, too fast), invasion (technology blurring boundaries around personal time), complexity (feeling inadequate to master the tool), and insecurity (fear of being replaced by the technology or by colleagues who adopt it faster). AI has given this decades-old concept new relevance and new intensity.

A 2024 peer-reviewed study published in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, following 416 professionals across three time points, offers a precise and genuinely useful finding: AI adoption does not directly cause burnout. Instead, it operates through a pathway — AI adoption increases job stress, and that elevated stress is what drives burnout. Critically, the study identifies self-efficacy in learning AI (a person's confidence in their own ability to learn and use the technology) as a protective factor that weakens this entire pathway. Employees who feel confident they can learn the tools experience significantly less stress from AI adoption in the first place.

### Why most organizations get this backwards

Recall the Deloitte finding from the AI transition overview: 59% of organizations take a tech-focused approach to AI rollout, and these organizations are 1.6 times more likely to miss their expected returns. Read alongside the self-efficacy research, this is close to a diagnosis: tools without structured, hands-on skill-building leave employees to develop confidence (or anxiety) on their own, unevenly, and usually slower than the pace of rollout — precisely the gap in which stress and, eventually, quiet resistance to the tools takes hold.

The practical implication is straightforward, if often skipped under deployment pressure: budget for real training time, not just tool licenses, and treat "employees are confident using this" as a rollout milestone with the same seriousness as "the tool is technically live."
