Harvard Business School professor Amy Edmondson, who has spent much of her career researching this topic, defines psychological safety as the belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking — that a person can ask a question, admit a mistake, raise a concern, or challenge an idea without fear of embarrassment, blame, or retaliation. It is a property of the team or organization, not a personality trait of the individual: the same person may speak up freely on one team and stay silent on another, depending entirely on what that environment has taught them is safe.

### Why it is a performance issue, not a comfort issue

When employees don't feel safe to speak up, organizations lose access to the information they most need: early warnings about problems, dissenting views that catch flawed decisions before they ship, and honest reporting of mistakes while they are still cheap to fix. Edmondson's research links low psychological safety directly to suppressed innovation and, in the most serious cases, to catastrophic failures that were visible to employees long before they reached leadership — but went unreported.

### How leaders build it deliberately

Psychological safety is not created by simply telling people it's "safe to speak up." Edmondson's research points to specific, repeatable leadership behaviors: framing work as a learning problem rather than a test of competence, so mistakes are treated as information rather than failure; actively inviting participation through genuine, open-ended questions that welcome disagreement; and responding productively when someone does speak up, with appreciation rather than defensiveness, even when the news is unwelcome.

### The manager effect

This is also where trust and psychological safety connect to hard business metrics: Gallup's workforce research attributes roughly 70% of the variance in team engagement to the manager alone. Safety and trust are built or destroyed largely in day-to-day interactions with a direct manager — which makes manager capability, not company-wide policy, the highest-leverage place to intervene.
